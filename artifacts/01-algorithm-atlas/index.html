01 / THE LEARNING LANDSCAPE

Different methods. Different jobs.

Machine learning methods can be organized by learning style (how they learn) and domain (what kind of problem or data they address). Both perspectives matter when choosing a model.

LEARNING STYLE / 01

Supervised

Train on labeled examples to predict a known target, such as a number or class.

Regression · Classification
LEARNING STYLE / 02

Unsupervised

Find structure in data without manually supplied outcome labels.

Clustering · Dimensionality reduction
LEARNING STYLE / 03

Self-supervised & hybrid

Construct targets from input data during pretraining; may later use supervised fine-tuning.

Transformers · Denoising generation

04 / SPOTLIGHT ON GENERATIVE AI

From recognizing patterns to creating content.

Discriminative systems often estimate labels, classes, or quantities. Generative systems learn structure in existing data to produce new samples or sequences. Their outputs still require evaluation for accuracy, bias, provenance, and safety.

01

GPT-style Transformer

Predicts a next token repeatedly to generate text.

02

Diffusion Model

Learns to reverse noise to synthesize images.

03

GAN

Uses a generator and discriminator in competition.

05 / PUTTING IT INTO PRACTICE

A quick selection guide.

START

What is the task?

Start with the data, target, and end-user decision—not with the most fashionable model.

HAVE LABELS?

Predict a known outcome

For tabular risk or demand prediction, start with linear models, decision trees, random forests, or boosting.

NO LABELS?

Discover a structure

Use K-means to group similar records or PCA to reduce dimensions.

COMPLEX INPUT?

Work with vision or language

Consider CNNs / BERT for recognition and GPT / diffusion / GANs for generation.

✳

Responsible ML is part of model selection. Across domains, evaluate data quality, privacy, fairness, explainability, validation strategy, and the cost of errors before real-world use.

06 / LEARNING REFLECTION

What I learned from the comparison.

A personal reflection on classification, model selection, and communicating technical concepts.

Creating this visual framework helped me realize that choosing an AI or machine learning approach requires more than recognizing popular algorithm names. I learned to compare models along two different dimensions: how they learn from data and what kinds of problems they are designed to solve. For instance, supervised methods such as Decision Trees and Random Forests can predict labeled outcomes, while K-means and PCA are more useful for uncovering structure without outcome labels.

The most interesting part was studying generative AI. GPT-style Transformers, diffusion models, and GANs demonstrate that machine learning is not limited to prediction or classification. They can also generate new content, although their training procedures do not always fit neatly into a simple supervised-versus-unsupervised distinction. Understanding self-supervised pretraining helped me communicate that nuance more accurately.

In my work with financial data, model selection needs to account for interpretability, data quality, and the consequences of inaccurate predictions. This assignment strengthened my ability to explain those tradeoffs to a nontechnical audience. Going forward, I want to apply this framework when comparing predictive methods for structured financial datasets while remaining mindful of bias, transparency, and responsible model validation.
